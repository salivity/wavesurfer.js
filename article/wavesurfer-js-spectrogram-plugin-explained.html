Wavesurfer.js Spectrogram Plugin Explained

This article provides an overview of the spectrogram plugin for Wavesurfer.js, detailing its purpose, the visual data it renders, and its practical applications. By converting raw audio into a detailed visual representation of frequency over time, this plugin expands the standard waveform view into a comprehensive acoustic analysis tool.

What the Spectrogram Displays

While the default Wavesurfer.js interface displays an amplitude waveform—showing loudness variations over time—the spectrogram plugin visualizes the frequency spectrum of an audio signal. It translates three primary dimensions of sound data into a two-dimensional color map:

  • Time (Horizontal Axis): The x-axis represents the progression of time, synchronized directly with the primary audio waveform and playback cursor.
  • Frequency (Vertical Axis): The y-axis represents the frequency range of the sound, measured in Hertz (Hz). Lower frequencies (such as bass tones) appear near the bottom of the display, while higher frequencies (treble or high-pitched harmonics) are positioned toward the top.
  • Amplitude and Intensity (Color Scale): Instead of using physical height to indicate volume, the spectrogram uses color gradients. Bright or high-contrast colors indicate high energy and louder frequency bands, while darker or cooler colors represent low energy or silence.

How It Works

The plugin relies on the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm provided by the Web Audio API. It continuously samples discrete slices of the audio signal, converting time-domain data into frequency-domain data. The developer can configure settings such as FFT size (which balances time resolution versus frequency resolution), frequency cutoffs, and custom color maps to tailor the visualization to specific sound profiles.

Primary Use Cases

Displaying a spectrogram is essential in applications where understanding the internal texture and frequency makeup of an audio file is necessary. Common implementations include:

  • Speech and Phonetics Analysis: Visualizing formants, vocal pitch, and consonant friction in linguistics or language-learning applications.
  • Audio Mastering and Restoration: Identifying unwanted low-frequency rumbles, electrical hums, sibilance, or high-frequency background noise that are invisible on a standard waveform.
  • Bioacoustics: Analyzing animal vocalizations, such as bird songs or marine mammal calls, which have distinct frequency signatures.
  • Music Production: Inspecting note pitches, harmonic overtones, and instrument frequency separation across a track.
